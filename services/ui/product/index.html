Why now

The control screen is becoming government-furnished. The record is not.

Programs now ask for open architectures and furnish their own control software. Units buy small drones from marketplaces, by the package, from several vendors. Airframes are reconfigured in the field. Whoever owns the ground station no longer owns the customer.

01

Fleets are mixed

A brigade flies three vendors' airframes on the same day. Readiness, spares and training have to roll up across all of them, with each vendor's limits stated plainly.

02

Buying is a storefront

A quote has to price the airframe, the record, the training and the spares in one package, with a delivery date the factory can actually make.

03

As-built goes stale

Field swaps and conversions change what each serial is. Without a live configuration record, the next loss autopsy is guessing.

So Drone Mission Cloud owns the part that stays valuable: the record and the sale around every drone, whoever made it, on any screen the unit already runs.

The platform

Operate, keep the record, sell the next lot.

Three products on one record. Every page ends in an action with an owner, scoped to the tenant that owns the data.

One operator, many drones, and the console says who needs you.

Vehicles that need the operator sort to the top with the reason. The map works air-gapped on baked terrain. Brief turns intent into a plan a human approves.

  • Exceptions firstLink lost, GNSS rejected by the spoof gate, low battery. J/K to walk them, ⌘K to jump anywhere.
  • Contested by defaultThe jamming zone, each VIO error circle and the CBBA task re-plan when a vehicle drops.
  • Launch readinessGo or no-go for every airframe on the pad, with the first failing check and the power-on-to-ready time.
  • Brief, approve, debriefPlain intent becomes a plan; a second person approves; the replay is deterministic.

The physics sits on the page where the decision is made.

Small drones are lost to the radio link behind a ridge, a pack that collapses in the cold, a gust they cannot out-thrust and wind the forecast never mentioned. Each plan is flown through all of it before launch, and every finding comes with a fix that changes the plan.

  • Launch checks with fixesA relay on the ridge, a higher leg, a band the jammer misses, a pack preheat or a bigger airframe, applied to the plan in one press.
  • Wind over terrainSpeed-up over crests and sinking air on lee slopes, priced per tail in watt-hours and thrust in hand.
  • Camera height on BriefPixels on a person, strip width and the ground hidden by trees at each height, set before approval.
  • Wear by flight loadBearing life and arm fatigue from how each tail was flown, not just its hours, with a ground-for-check action.

A per-serial record from the factory bench to the field loss, and back.

Each airframe is born on the end-of-line bench with its lots, calibrations and firmware hash. Field sorties, swaps and losses attach to that serial, so a bad lot is found in days.

  • Birth record at the benchRead-only MAVLink at end of line; a failed hover test grounds the serial before it ships.
  • Attrition autopsyRanks lots by loss ratio and puts a quality hold on the tails that carry them.
  • Field fixesA corrective change closes only when the treated unit beats the control units by a measured margin.
  • Stockpile healthOver-age packs hold their airframe from ready until they are rotated.

Quote, bid and surge from the same record the fleet runs on.

Sales stops guessing. Sortie cost comes from end-of-flight records, delivery dates from the factory's real capacity, and bid answers link to records that exist.

  • Listing and quotePrice equals its lines, and nobody can commit a date faster than the factory can build.
  • Bid deskEach "met" requirement links to its evidence; out-of-scope rows say so instead of overclaiming.
  • Surge plannerThe plain date never beats the slowest part. Expediting and a second shift show their cost.
  • All vendorsReadiness across every vendor's tails; imports check every row and say what they could not verify.

Allied buyers get their own tenant. Releasability is enforced at the API.

An allied session never receives a field its rules withhold, at any depth of any response. The page shows what was withheld and why, so nobody mistakes a gap for missing data.

  • Tenant hierarchy and sharingCustomers see their own fleet and nothing of other customers or the maker's internals.
  • Two-person sharingLoss data flows to the maker only while the customer shares it, and they can turn it off.
  • Hash-chained auditEvery allow and deny, with the reason, in a tamper-evident log.
  • Air-gap readyNo runtime SaaS, baked terrain, FIPS crypto and signed behavior packages.

A second market on the same record

A police drone program runs on it too: every launch approved, every flight on the record.

Agencies are putting docked drones on rooftops and piers and staffing them with contract remote pilots. Each call raises the same questions a military fleet does: who approved the launch, who can call it off, what does the record say, and what do we pay for. The same platform answers them for a public safety customer in its own operating mode, with its own words and its own records policy.

  1. DispatchA call comes in

    It lands in the queue with its priority, incident number and how long it has waited.

  2. Supervisor decidesApprove the launch

    The nearest dock whose aircraft passes its checks is picked by flight time. Nothing flies without this approval.

  3. Pilot decidesFly it, or don't

    The pilot can decline or end any flight for safety. No role can override that call.

  4. Pilot filesThe record writes itself

    Prefilled from the flight; the pilot adds what happened. A correction is a new version on a hash chain.

  5. Program manager decidesPay for the month

    The invoice is built from signed shift logs. Unstaffed hours and hours a pilot worked while not current don't bill.

The shift, the roster and the records request, run from the same evidence.

The program manager knows the console is staffed by a pilot who is current. The contractor bills for hours that were really worked. When a records request or a lawsuit arrives, the custodian can show who decided what.

  • The shift gates the launchStart-of-shift checks on every dock, the weather and the airspace before the first approval. At hand-off the pilot signs a log built from the shift's own records.
  • Only current pilots on the rosterEach pilot's certificate, recurrent training, TWIC, CJIS and supervised time. A lapsed item can't be rostered, and a new pilot flies solo only after the program manager signs them off.
  • Cranes and bridges in the pre-flightCrane rows, container stacks and bridge towers are on the map. A route that crosses one says how high to climb.
  • Mishaps on a clockVerbal at once, written within 24 hours, with the FAA reportability test applied.
  • Records that hold upRetention by record type, legal holds, and records requests released with plates, phones and names redacted and a hash manifest.
  • A contractor seat that sees only its partThe pilot company keeps its pilots' files and submits the month. It never sees the live console or incident records.

Open problems

What is not proven yet

  • Tracker v2 holds the target in 3 of 5 SITL runs; in the other 2 it swaps to a parked look-alike.
  • The end-of-line bench has run only against a loopback emulator. It needs a real PX4 board, and its limits are placeholders.
  • Prices, orders, customers and loss data are simulated. The adapter conformance test has not met a real non-PX4 vehicle.

Guardrails

Built to be bought by people who have to sign for it.

Fire control stays on the airframe

The platform keeps the record and supervises the fleet; it never arms, targets or fires. Strike airframes are fully supported as products in the catalog, the record and the quote (ADR 0016).

Humans approve tasking

The console monitors and advises. Plans need a second approver, and GO at launch is advice; nothing arms or launches. In a first responder program a supervisor approves each launch and the pilot's safety call stands.

Outbound only

CoT for TAK and entity feeds go out. Inbound commands are refused and written to the audit log.

Releasability at the API

Allied tenants never receive withheld fields. Classification banners and portion marks on every page.

Hash-chained audit

Every allow and deny, with its reason codes, in a tamper-evident chain an assessor can verify.

Air-gap ready

No runtime SaaS, terrain baked offline, FIPS crypto, signed behavior packages and an air-gap install bundle.

Pricing

Priced into the drone, not per seat.

The record travels with the airframe, so it is priced with the airframe. Fleets pay per formation, so adding drones never adds friction.
