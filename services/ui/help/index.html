Getting started

Drone Mission Cloud is one platform with two sides: the manufacturer runs its operations, and each customer runs its own fleet. The hosted demo plays a scripted sortie on a real range in California's central coast, with nine vehicles in the air and three on the pad.

  1. Open the console and sign in with the account you were given (see demo accounts).
  2. A customer account opens on Operate. A manufacturer account opens on the Work queue.
  3. Press ⌘K (or Ctrl K) to jump to any vehicle, plan, page or filter. Press ? for the shortcut sheet. Walk the demo in ⌘K opens a guided walk through the loop; it opens by itself on a first visit from an invite link.
  4. Take an action, then sign out and sign in as the other side to see its effect. Demo state lives in your browser tab, so it carries across accounts and survives a reload. A new tab starts fresh, and Reset the demo data in ⌘K clears it.
Every page shows UNCLASSIFIED // DEMO DATA in the banner, and every simulated figure carries a SIM label.

What's new

The newest things in the console, with the full list in the repository's CHANGELOG.md.

  • A first responder program. A public safety customer flies docked aircraft from a remote console, on the same platform. Calls come in from dispatch, a supervisor approves each launch, the nearest ready dock is picked by flight time, and the pilot flies it. The pilot can decline or end any flight for safety and no one can override that. Every flight gets a record prefilled from the flight, written once and corrected only by a new version sealed beside the original. Sign in as supervisor or pilot, or switch to it from the admin's workspace menu. See First responder program.
  • Each role's journey, tested end to end. A bot for every demo account walked its journey from this site through sign-in and pressed every button on every page, on a laptop and a phone. What it found is fixed: a customer no longer sees other customers on Releasability, an allied tenant sees its fleet as its own, a button your role cannot use is greyed out with the reason instead of failing, empty tables say what to do next, and drawers and the palette close with Esc.
  • Energy, radio shadow and pack ageing. Debrief shows where each vehicle's energy went (lift, drag, climb, wind, cold) and its link margin over the terrain along the flown track. Operate has a Radio shadow layer for where the control link goes thin behind terrain. Stockpile health charts each stored pack's capacity and what a storage charge or a cooler store buys.
  • Wind over terrain, camera height and wear. Missions has a Wind view on the plan map: where the terrain speeds the forecast wind up and where the air sinks on a lee slope, with what that costs each vehicle. Brief shows what each camera sees of each task by height, and a commander can set the height before approving. Airframes shows each tail's wear by how it was flown, not only its hours, and lets a maintainer ground it for a check.
  • Terrain, cold packs and thrust, on the pages that use them. Each plan in Missions is flown through the terrain between it and the GCS, a battery that sags in the cold and the thrust left in a gust, in the conditions you set; findings sit in Launch checks with a fix (a relay on the ridge, a higher leg, a band the jammer misses, a pack preheat, a different airframe) that goes into the plan. The Loadout planner shows packs and thrust for every airframe, the Denial map shows where the control and video links hold, and Airframes shows what a tail's next sortie needs on the pad.
  • Guided walk. A card that steps a first visitor through the loop: work queue, quality hold, the customer's fleet, Brief, Operate, Debrief and the audit chain. Open it from ⌘K with Walk the demo.
  • ⌘K finds things. Every tail and every plan in the workspace is a palette entry and opens selected on its page.
  • Access as a document. Who can read and do what in the demo is a generated table that a test holds the code to: see the developer site.
  • Faster first open. Each page loads the first time it opens, so the console's entry script is a fifth of its former size.
  • Accessibility. WCAG 2.1 A/AA checks run on fifteen pages in CI; the contrast and keyboard findings they caught are fixed.
  • One physics model. Momentum-theory power, a LiPo curve and gusting wind drive the sortie, launch checks, debrief energy and the loadout planner.

The two workspaces

Your organization decides your workspace, and your role shapes the menu on the left: each role lands on its own home and sees the pages it works in, in the order its work runs. Every other page in the workspace is still one ⌘K away. A red count beside a page means something there is waiting on you.

Manufacturer workspace

Work queue, then Deliver (Contract to field, Program), Make (Quality, Compliance, Configuration, Software updates), Sell (Quotes, Surge planner, Bid desk, Pricing plans, Releasability) and Learn (Field fixes, Attrition, Field reports, Evidence, Loadout planner). The manufacturer never sees a customer's people or another customer's fleet, and sees loss data only while that customer shares it.

Customer workspace

Fly (Operate, Brief, Missions, Debrief, Loadout planner, Denial map), Fleet (Airframes, All vendors, Configuration, Stockpile health, Crew readiness), Supply (Receiving, Accountability, Requests to Neros, Order, Software updates) and Learn (Attrition, Field reports). Operators see the flying pages; maintainers start on Airframes and see the fleet and supply pages first. The manufacturer's internals return a refusal, not an empty page.

What flows between them

  • Requests from the customer: airframes, spares, training and repairs.
  • Shipments from the maker, each with its birth record and packet.
  • Loss data to the maker, only while the customer has sharing on.

What is simulated

Read every number by its label:

  • PX4 SITL PX4 v1.18 flies in SIH with its own EKF2, and the onboard code closes the loop over MAVLink.
  • Unit check The onboard C module runs open loop against a synthetic or recorded stream.
  • SIM Demo scenario data: fleets, customers, orders, prices, lots, losses and training records.
  • SIM The physics: power, battery, wind over terrain, radio links, thrust, camera optics, pack ageing and wear. The terrain is real (SRTM); every coefficient is an assumption written out in the source, not a Neros figure or a flight-test result.

Manufacturer catalog items appear as published. Other vendors appear under generic labels, never real product names. Prices are planning assumptions, not anyone's price list.

The Operate screen

One screen for one operator and many vehicles. Left to right: the vehicle list, the tactical picture, the flight display for the selected vehicle, and the event timeline along the bottom.

  • The list sorts by need. Vehicles that need you rise to the top with the reason: link lost, GNSS rejected by the spoof gate, low battery, grounded.
  • The map works air-gapped. Terrain is baked offline. Overlays show survey coverage, the jamming zone, each VIO error circle, link quality and the current task allocation. Every overlay is labeled SIM.
  • 3D shows the same picture in relief, each vehicle at its height above ground and the jamming zone as a dome (heights ×2).
  • Radio shadow. Each vehicle's link quality is its margin to the ground station over the terrain, not distance alone. The Radio shadow layer shades where control at 100 m goes thin or is lost behind a ridge.
  • Bingo. Every vehicle flies one performance model: power against airspeed, a pack that sags under load, a gusting wind. When the reserve would not survive the rest of the plan it turns for home, and the list says how many minutes it has left against minutes home. In the demo NX-06 bingoes and lands.
  • Replay. Drag the timeline, or use ← →, to see what the console received at any moment.
  • Field report. Select a vehicle and press R to file what you saw. Reports feed the fleet's record.

Add ?fleet=300 to the console URL for the fleet-scale view.

Launch readiness

Press L to see the vehicles on the ground. Each gets a go or no-go from four checks, and the row shows the first one that fails, so you know what to fix without opening anything:

  1. A fresh link.
  2. Airworthiness from the fleet record (a failed end-of-line test or an open work order fails it).
  3. Battery at or above 60%.
  4. A position fix: trusted GNSS, or VIO.

The console times each vehicle from power-on to GO and keeps the median. In the demo, NX-12 stays NO-GO because its bench test failed, and NX-11 sits at 41% battery.

GO is advice. The console does not arm or launch anything.

Jamming, VIO and lost link

GNSS rejected. When the spoof gate rejects GNSS, the vehicle navigates on visual-inertial odometry. The map draws its position error as a circle that grows with distance flown (an assumed 1% in the demo). NX-04 orbits inside the jamming zone to show it.

Lost link. A vehicle that stops reporting goes stale after 20 seconds and rises to the top of the list. Its tasks go back to the fleet, and the remaining vehicles re-plan with CBBA. NX-07 drops off the link about every 100 seconds in the demo.

Denial map. The Denial map page builds a picture of where GNSS was denied or rejected from repeated passes, so the next plan can route around it. Its Control link and Video link layers show the margin from the ground-station mast to every 200 m cell at the height, band and jammer state you choose.

FlyCommander

Brief and two-person approval

  1. On Brief, type the mission in plain words, for example "Search the north field with two vehicles before 14:00, then check the culvert, keep two in reserve".
  2. The console proposes a plan: areas, vehicles, reserves, and which vehicles it left out and why (low battery, grounded, on hold).
  3. A planner or mission commander approves it. The person who wrote the brief cannot approve their own plan.

Camera height. Before approving, the commander sees what each assigned camera gets of each task at each height: pixels on a person, the width of a survey strip, the ground hidden behind trees and buildings, and the video margin back to the station. The height they pick is filed with the plan.

The parser is a fixed grammar that runs offline. There is no language model in the loop, so the same words always give the same plan.

Operators can read a Brief but cannot approve one. The refusal is written to the audit log.

Missions, launch checks and debrief

Missions lists every plan by stage (draft, review, approved, active, closed) with its launch checks, tied to the same fleet record as Operate. Pick a plan to see why it is a go or a no-go.

  • Conditions at launch. Set the air temperature, wind, wind direction, gusts and whether an EW emitter is on. It is a what-if until a planner saves it, and every check below re-runs in that air.
  • Launch checks with fixes. Besides the fleet record (a grounded tail, a low pack, a two-person rule), each plan is flown through the terrain, a pack that sags in the cold and the thrust left in a gust. A finding comes with a fix you can apply: a relay on the ridge, a higher leg, a band the jammer misses, a pack preheat, a bigger pack or a different airframe. Applying it changes the plan, and the checks run again.
  • The plan map. Switch between Routes, Video link (the link colored over the terrain, with the worst terrain profile) and Wind (where the terrain speeds the forecast wind up, where the air sinks on a lee slope, and what that costs each tail).
  • Packs and thrust. Each tail's cell voltage through the sortie, preheat advice and thrust in hand.

In the demo, M-1034 (Road junction overwatch) is the no-go to open: a low pack, a grounded tail and plans that need more energy than the packs give.

Debrief replays a mission deterministically and exports the timeline with its marking. Where the energy went splits each tail's watt-hours into lift, blades, drag, climb, wind and cold, and the link margin is plotted over the terrain along the flown track, so you can tell whether a drop-out was the terrain.

RecordMaintainer

Airframes and birth records

Every airframe has a birth record from the end-of-line bench: its serial, the lot of every major part, its calibrations, its firmware hash and the bench test result. The record decides airworthiness, so a failed hover test grounds the serial before it ships.

Field events attach to the same serial: receiving, sorties, swaps, reports, losses. That is what lets the attrition autopsy find a bad lot.

Wear by flight load. A tail flown hard wears faster than its hours say. For the selected tail the page shows each motor's bearing life and the arm fatigue from hard landings against a flat hours schedule, and a maintainer can ground it for a check.

Next sortie. What the tail's next plan needs on the pad: preheat, link and thrust, with a link to the plan.

RecordMaintainer

Quality holds and work orders

A lot hold restricts every tail that carries the lot. An open work order blocks return to service. To clear a vehicle:

  1. Open Airframes and pick the restricted tail.
  2. Close its open work orders. Parts come from the maker's depot stock.
  3. Return it to service. Launch readiness picks up the change at once.

Closing a work order needs the maintainer, mission commander or admin role.

Attrition and the fix loop

Attrition ranks lots by their loss rate against the fleet baseline and proposes a quality hold on the tails that carry a flagged lot. In the demo, ESC lot ESC-2611 loses airframes at 2.14× the expected rate.

Field fixes tracks the corrective change. A case closes only when the treated unit improves against the control units by a measured margin (a difference in differences with z ≤ −1.28 and at least 40 sorties a side). A change that does nothing never closes.

Public safety

First responder program

Customer D is a harbor police program that flies docked aircraft as first responders. It runs on the same platform as the defense customers, in its own operating mode: its own menu (Respond, Flight records, Pilots and roster, Report and invoice), its own words (flight, supervisor, call) and its own records policy (the pilot's name is on every record). Everything in it is simulated: the harbor is a schematic and the docks, pilots, calls and weather are made up.

A call to a launch

  1. Dispatch sends a call. It lands in Calls waiting with its priority, incident number and how long it has waited. Next call from dispatch sends the next scripted call.
  2. The supervisor approves. The console picks the dock with the shortest flight time whose aircraft passes its checks (charged to 60%, dock powered and communicating, energy for the round trip with a 20% reserve). The supervisor can pick another dock, change the priority, or stand the request down.
  3. The pilot launches. Pre-flight checks run again for that dock: aircraft, dock, energy, wind against the waiver limits, NOTAMs and TFRs, link. The pilot never launches without an approval.
  4. The pilot's safety call stands. The pilot can decline a call or end a flight at any point, with a reason. No role has an override: the supervisor's screen has a Release to dock button, not a "keep flying" one. On the water rescue call the gusts rise past the 12 m/s waiver limit after arrival, to show it.
  5. Privacy. The supervisor can tell the pilot to stop observing: the camera goes to the horizon and the record says when.

The flight record

Every flight gets a record due before the shift ends. Launch and recovery times, aircraft, dock, incident location and number, reason, approver, pilot, weather and airspace come from the flight; the pilot writes the narrative and any anomaly. A filed record is never edited. A correction is a new version with a reason, kept beside the original, and every version is hashed onto the one before it, so a changed record breaks the chain. Requests not flown are listed with who decided and why.

The shift

  1. Start-of-shift checks. Before the first launch the pilot runs the checks on Respond: each dock and aircraft, weather against the waiver limits, NOTAMs and TFRs, the supervisor and radio. A dock problem is reported to the supervisor and logged when the shift starts. Nothing is approved or launched until a pilot has started the shift.
  2. Tall structures. The map shows the crane rows, container stacks and bridge towers. When a route crosses one, the pre-flight says how high the transit climbs to clear it.
  3. Mishaps. The pilot reports a mishap to the supervisor at once and writes it up within 24 hours; the console counts down. The written report says whether it goes to the FAA: a serious injury or more than $500 of damage to other property, within 10 days.
  4. The shift log. At the end of the shift the pilot reads a log built from the shift's own records (hours staffed, flights, requests not flown, gaps, discrepancies, records still to file) and signs it. The signed log is sealed into the same evidence chain as the flight records, and the console passes to the next rostered pilot.

Pilots and roster

The program manager (the supervisor seat) keeps one pilot on console twelve hours a day for the next 14 days. Each pilot's file shows their certificate, recurrent training, hours, background, TWIC and CJIS, and supervised time, with what falls due within 30 days. A pilot with a lapsed item can't be rostered. A pilot who isn't yet signed off works only with a supervising pilot, and the program manager signs them off for solo shifts once they have three supervised shifts and ten supervised flights. Empty days and problem days are flagged, with the hours they cost.

The month

  • Report and invoice. The contractor's monthly activity report and invoice are both built from the signed shift logs: calls, flights, call-to-scene times, hours staffed against scheduled, gaps, mishaps, currency and aircraft care. Unstaffed hours are not payable, nor are hours a pilot worked while a credential had lapsed. A shift whose flight record isn't filed is held until it is. The contractor submits the month and the program manager approves it or returns it with a note. Both steps are sealed into the evidence chain.
  • Battery and aircraft care. Each aircraft gets a weekly check signed by the pilot on Flight records: pack health, cell spread, swelling, propellers, motors and dock. An overdue check shows as a caution on the console. A pack under 80% health, over 30 mV spread, or swollen grounds the aircraft until a passing check is signed.
  • Retention and legal holds. Every record shows how long it is kept (flight records 2 years, shift logs 3, mishap reports 5, SIM policy). The supervisor, as records custodian, can place a legal hold with a matter number, which stops the clock until it is lifted.
  • Records requests. The custodian gathers the filed records for an incident number or a date range. Plates, phone numbers and private persons' names are redacted, and the package is reviewed and then released with a manifest. The manifest names each sealed version by its hash, and the release itself goes on the chain.
  • The contractor seat. Sign in as contractor: the pilot company keeps its pilots' files current (a TWIC renewal makes A. Reyes rosterable again), sees the roster, and submits the month. It never sees the live console or the incident records, and the pilots never see the invoice.
Sign in as supervisor and as pilot to see each seat on its own. The admin's workspace menu opens the program as Neros, holding both seats so the walk works from one login. The access matrix holds each real login to its own seat.
Trust

What the console will never do

The platform has no weapon, payload-release or fire-control type in its data model (ADR 0016). Strike airframes appear only as catalog items.
  • It never arms, launches or commands a vehicle. It reads MAVLink and sends no MAVLink commands.
  • It accepts no commands from external systems. Feeds to TAK and other C2 systems go out only; an inbound command is refused and audited.
  • Outcomes are neutral: completed, aborted, lost. There are no target, strike or damage fields.
  • Tracking means an operator-designated object, in simulation, with confirm, abort and timeout written to the audit log.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

How do I reset the demo?

Press ⌘K and choose Reset the demo data, or open a new tab. A reload keeps what you did, so you can come back to it; your sign-in lasts 12 hours.

Why did an action say "role_denied"?

Your role cannot do it. That is the point of the demo: sign in as the role that can, and the refusal stays in the audit log.

Does it work on a phone?

Yes for reading and acting on queues, plans and the fleet: the menu folds into a drawer and the guided walk starts folded so it does not cover the page. The Operate screen is built for a laptop or a ground-station display.

Is this a Neros product?

No. It is an independent concept by Kevin Owens. Manufacturer catalog items appear as published; everything else is simulated.

Where is the API?

See the developer site, which has a live API explorer that runs against the demo.
